Distraction Free YouTube vs CleanFeed (2026)

One is a free, featherweight pair of toggles. The other enforces focus when willpower runs out. Here's exactly where each one wins.

Updated June 2026 · Written by the CleanFeed team.

If you searched for "Distraction Free YouTube vs CleanFeed," you probably already have a free, minimal extension installed and you're wondering whether anything paid is actually worth it. That's the right question to ask. Distraction Free YouTube does one thing well: it strips a handful of the noisiest elements off YouTube with almost no setup and no cost. CleanFeed is a much larger toolkit built around a different goal — enforcing focus, not just decorating the page. This comparison walks through both honestly, including the cases where you should skip CleanFeed entirely and keep what you already have.

Full disclosure: I'm Daniel, I built CleanFeed. I've also used Distraction Free YouTube for months. This comparison aims to be honest, including the cases where Distraction Free YouTube is the better choice. The links are not affiliate links.

The short version: if you want two or three things hidden and you never want to think about an extension again, Distraction Free YouTube is genuinely excellent and it costs nothing. If you keep turning your own blockers back on at 11pm, or you want to measure how much time you actually spend, that's where CleanFeed earns its place — and its one-time $4.99.

It helps to name the trap most "distraction-free" tools fall into. They hide the noise, which feels like progress, but they leave the door unlocked. The homepage feed is gone until the moment you want it back, and then it's one toggle away. For people who genuinely just dislike the clutter, that's perfect — the door never needs to be locked because they never try to open it. For people fighting a habit, an unlocked door is the entire problem. Keep that distinction in mind as you read; it decides which tool you should pick far more than any feature checklist does.

When Distraction Free YouTube is the better choice

I want to be clear: for a large share of people, Distraction Free YouTube is the smarter pick, and recommending it costs me a customer. Here's when it wins.

You only want a couple of things gone. If your entire problem is "hide the homepage feed" or "kill the related-video sidebar," a minimal extension solves it in one click and gets out of your way. There's nothing to configure, nothing to learn, and no popup full of switches you'll never touch. Simplicity is a feature, and Distraction Free YouTube treats it as the main feature.

You're allergic to paying for browser extensions. Totally fair. Distraction Free YouTube is free, and "free and good enough" beats "paid and slightly better" for most casual users. If you'd resent a $4.99 charge — even a one-time one — for something you use lightly, you should not buy CleanFeed. Use the free tool with a clear conscience.

You don't have a self-control problem with YouTube — you have a clutter problem. This is the key distinction. If you can already close the tab when you mean to, and you just find the interface noisy, you don't need enforcement. You need a tidier page. Distraction Free YouTube gives you exactly that with the lowest possible footprint, and adding Focus Lock or Pomodoro on top would just be weight you never use.

When CleanFeed is the better choice

CleanFeed exists for the opposite situation: when hiding elements isn't enough because the moment you're motivated, you just unhide them again. Three things separate it.

Focus Lock. You set a 4-digit PIN and your chosen blockers lock on. To turn one off mid-session you have to hold a button for a full 60 seconds — long enough that the impulse usually passes. The PIN is stored as a salted SHA-256 hash, never in plaintext. A toggle you can flip in half a second cannot do this; that's not a knock on Distraction Free YouTube, it's a different category of tool.

The built-in Pomodoro timer. Run a 25/5 cycle and every blocker auto-locks during the focus block, then relaxes on the break. Distraction Free YouTube has no concept of time or sessions — it's the same page all day.

A time tracker that only counts focused, visible YouTube tabs. Not background tabs, not music left playing in another window. If you've ever been shocked by your screen-time number, seeing the real figure is its own intervention. And the $4.99 is a single lifetime payment — no subscription, no account, no telemetry — so the math is easy: one coffee, forever.

Detailed feature breakdown

Blocking coverage

Distraction Free YouTube focuses on the obvious offenders — typically the homepage feed and the sidebar of recommended videos — and hides them cleanly. That covers the 80% case for a lot of people. CleanFeed takes the same idea much further with 17 individual blockers: Shorts, the homepage feed, sidebar recommendations, comments, autoplay, end-screen suggestions, trending and explore tabs, live chat, thumbnails, the algorithmic subscription feed, the merch shelf, breaking-news cards, mixes and playlists, Playables, and more. Each one is a separate switch, so you can carve YouTube down to exactly the surface you want rather than accepting a preset. If you only need two of those, the free version of CleanFeed covers it; if you want all 17 locked together, that's the paid tier.

Enforcement vs. decoration

This is the real dividing line. Distraction Free YouTube changes how the page looks. CleanFeed can change what you're able to do. With Focus Lock engaged, the 60-second hold-to-disable means a relapse takes deliberate, sustained effort instead of a reflex click. If your YouTube problem is willpower rather than aesthetics, this single difference is the whole reason to switch. If it's not, you can safely ignore it.

Targeting specific content

CleanFeed adds tools Distraction Free YouTube doesn't aim to offer: a channel whitelist so trusted creators always show, a right-click blocklist to nuke channels you never want to see, and keyword filtering that hides videos by words in their title. Per-page rules let you set each blocker to On, Off, or Inherit on each type of YouTube page — strict on the homepage, relaxed on a subscription page, for instance. Power users can also drop in custom CSS. Distraction Free YouTube intentionally skips most of this to stay light, which is a legitimate design choice, not a flaw.

Setup and day-to-day friction

Here Distraction Free YouTube has a real edge worth stating plainly: there's almost nothing to set up. Install it, and the distractions are gone. CleanFeed has a popup with every blocker, a Pomodoro control, the time-tracker readout, and an options page for per-page rules, whitelists and custom CSS. That's more surface to learn, and if you only want a clean page it's more than you need. CleanFeed tries to keep the defaults sane so you can ignore the advanced parts, but I won't pretend a 17-blocker toolkit is as instantly invisible as a two-toggle extension. If "I never want to open a settings panel" is a hard requirement, that points toward the simpler tool.

Scope and platform

Both are desktop Chrome extensions that act only on YouTube — neither blocks the rest of the web, and neither runs on mobile. CleanFeed additionally works on YouTube Music and ships in 19 locales. It's open source under the MIT license and built by a solo developer, so you can read every line before trusting it. I'll be straight about one thing: CleanFeed is new on the Chrome Web Store with a small day-one user base. Distraction Free YouTube has been around longer. If a proven track record matters more to you than the feature set, weigh that honestly.

Pricing comparison

Distraction Free YouTube is free, and that's a real advantage — there's no breakeven to calculate because the cost is zero. CleanFeed is $4.99 paid once, for life: no subscription, no renewals, no account, no telemetry collecting your data to subsidize a "free" price. The honest way to frame it: you are not paying for blocking, because basic blocking is free in both tools (and CleanFeed gives you any 2 blockers free forever, plus the daily time tracker and a 1-hour pause). You'd only pay the $4.99 for the enforcement layer — Focus Lock, Pomodoro, and the full 17-blocker set locked together. If that layer saves you even one wasted evening, it has paid for itself permanently. If you'd never use it, don't buy it. Free should win when the paid features don't apply to you, and I'd rather you keep your $4.99 than spend it on switches you'll leave untouched.

Honest verdict

If you want a clean, lightweight, zero-cost way to hide a couple of YouTube distractions and never think about it again, Distraction Free YouTube is the right answer — it's free, it's simple, and adding anything more would just be clutter you don't need. Choose CleanFeed only if your problem is enforcement rather than appearance: if you keep flipping your own blockers back on, want a Pomodoro that locks the page while you work, or want to see exactly how much focused time YouTube is eating. Try CleanFeed's free tier first — two blockers and the time tracker cost nothing — and upgrade for $4.99 once only if Focus Lock turns out to be the thing you were actually missing.
