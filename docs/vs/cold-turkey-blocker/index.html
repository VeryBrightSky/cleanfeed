Cold Turkey vs CleanFeed: When to Use Which

A system-wide nuclear blocker and a surgical YouTube tool. They solve different problems — and they can happily run side by side.

Updated June 2026 · Written by the CleanFeed team.

Comparing Cold Turkey Blocker to CleanFeed is a little like comparing a circuit breaker to a dimmer switch. Cold Turkey is a desktop app that can shut off entire websites and applications across your whole operating system; CleanFeed is a Chrome extension that surgically removes the addictive surfaces inside YouTube while leaving the site fully usable. They are not really competitors — they are different categories of tool, and the honest answer to "which should I use" is often "it depends on whether you want to keep using the thing you're blocking."

This page lays out where each one wins, what they cost, and why a lot of people end up running both. If you want a total lockdown that you genuinely cannot wriggle out of, Cold Turkey is in a class of its own. If you need YouTube to stay open for work, research, lectures, or music — but without the Shorts rabbit hole and the recommendation sidebar pulling you under — that is exactly the gap CleanFeed was built to fill.

Full disclosure: I'm Daniel, I built CleanFeed. I've also used Cold Turkey Blocker for months. This comparison aims to be honest, including the cases where Cold Turkey Blocker is the better choice. The links are not affiliate links.

When Cold Turkey is the better choice

Cold Turkey Blocker is the right tool when "just YouTube" is not the problem — the problem is everything. Because it runs as a desktop application rather than a browser extension, it can block at the operating-system level: entire websites across every browser you have installed, plus native apps, games, even the whole internet if you want. CleanFeed simply cannot do that, and it does not pretend to.

Total lockdown. If you genuinely do not trust yourself with browser-only blocking — if your pattern is to switch from Chrome to Firefox, or open an Incognito window, or reach for the desktop app the moment willpower dips — Cold Turkey closes those exits. Its Pro lock options are deliberately hard to bypass: you can lock a block until a scheduled time, for a fixed duration, or behind a password you hand to someone else. That is a stronger commitment device than any browser extension can offer, including CleanFeed's Focus Lock.

Scheduled blackout periods. Cold Turkey's scheduler is excellent for recurring deep-work blocks: block all of social media, news, and YouTube every weekday from 9am to 12pm, automatically, forever. It treats your whole digital environment as one surface to control.

Beyond the browser. If your distractions are desktop games, a chat client, or a Steam library, an extension is useless and Cold Turkey is the obvious answer. For wholesale, cross-application discipline, $39 once is a fair price for what is arguably the strongest consumer blocker available.

When CleanFeed is the better choice

CleanFeed wins the moment you need to keep using YouTube. For a huge number of people YouTube is not optional — it is where the tutorials, the lectures, the documentation walkthroughs, the conference talks, and the background music live. Blocking it entirely with Cold Turkey is like banning the library because you keep getting distracted by the magazines near the door. CleanFeed instead removes only the magazines.

Surgical, not nuclear. Turn off Shorts, the homepage feed, the recommendation sidebar, autoplay, and end-screen suggestions — and what's left is a clean search-and-watch tool. You can look up exactly the video you came for, watch it, and leave, without the algorithm offering you ten more. Cold Turkey can only give you "YouTube on" or "YouTube off."

Focus Lock + Pomodoro. CleanFeed has its own commitment mechanism: a 4-digit PIN locks your blockers on, and disabling them mid-session requires a deliberate 60-second hold. The built-in Pomodoro timer (25/5) auto-locks every blocker during focus blocks. It is YouTube-scoped rather than system-wide, but it is real friction, built for exactly the surface where most people relapse.

The $4.99-once wedge. At $4.99 one-time versus Cold Turkey's $39, CleanFeed is roughly an eighth of the price — because it does roughly an eighth of the job, very precisely. If YouTube is your one weak spot, you don't need to pay for OS-wide lockdown you'll never use.

Detailed feature breakdown

Scope of blocking

This is the whole ballgame. Cold Turkey operates at the system level — it can block by domain, by application, and on a schedule, across your entire machine. That breadth is its superpower and also its bluntness: blocking is binary. A site is either reachable or it is not. CleanFeed operates inside one site, YouTube, and slices it into 17 separately togglable surfaces: Shorts, the homepage feed, the sidebar recommendations, comments, autoplay, end-screen suggestions, the trending and explore tabs, live chat, thumbnails, the subscription algorithm feed, the merch shelf, breaking-news cards, mixes and playlists, Playables, and more. You decide, surface by surface, what stays and what goes.

Commitment and lock

Both tools understand that the real enemy is your future self at a low-willpower moment. Cold Turkey's locks are the gold standard for sheer un-bypassability — once a block is locked for a duration or until a time, you are not getting around it without serious effort. CleanFeed's Focus Lock is lighter but genuinely effective for its scope: blockers lock behind a 4-digit PIN (stored salted-SHA256, never plaintext), and turning them off mid-session means holding a button for a full 60 seconds — long enough to interrupt the impulse. If absolute, "I literally cannot undo this" lockdown matters most to you, Cold Turkey wins this row. If you want meaningful friction scoped to YouTube, CleanFeed is plenty.

Productivity tools

CleanFeed bundles a Pomodoro timer (25-minute focus, 5-minute break) that auto-locks every blocker during focus intervals, plus a time tracker that only counts focused, visible YouTube tabs — never a video left playing in a background tab. Cold Turkey reports app and site usage but is a blocker first; it does not run a Pomodoro cycle inside your YouTube session. If you want your focus timer and your YouTube hygiene to be the same tool, CleanFeed does that natively.

Precision controls

CleanFeed adds a channel whitelist, a right-click blocklist, keyword filtering that hides videos by title, per-page rules (On/Off/Inherit for each blocker on each YouTube page), and custom CSS for power users. It also works on YouTube Music and ships in 19 locales. These are all things a system-level blocker has no concept of, because it never looks inside the page. Cold Turkey's equivalent strength is in the other direction: rules that span dozens of sites and apps at once.

Platform and portability

Cold Turkey is a desktop install (Windows and macOS) and protects that machine. CleanFeed is a Chrome extension, so it follows your Chrome profile across machines but does nothing outside the browser. Neither covers phones natively. Pick based on where your distraction actually happens.

Pricing comparison

Both tools are refreshingly free of subscriptions, which is rare in this category. Cold Turkey Blocker has a capable free version, and its Pro upgrade is a one-time $39 (with options to add it to more machines). CleanFeed is $4.99 one-time for lifetime access, with a free tier that keeps any 2 blockers active forever plus the daily time tracker and a 1-hour pause.

So the math is simple and there is no recurring-cost trap on either side: $39 once for system-wide control of your entire computer, or $4.99 once for surgical control of YouTube. CleanFeed is about one-eighth the price because it solves a deliberately narrower problem. If you only ever wanted YouTube tamed, paying the extra $34 for OS-wide blocking you won't use is poor value. If you need to lock down games, multiple browsers, and the wider web, $39 for Cold Turkey is a bargain by comparison. And because both are one-time purchases, buying both still costs under $44 total, forever — which is why running them together is a genuinely reasonable setup.

Honest verdict

If your distractions sprawl across your whole machine — multiple browsers, desktop apps, games — and you want a lock you fundamentally cannot bypass, buy Cold Turkey Blocker; nothing here beats it for total lockdown. If YouTube is your specific weak spot and you need it to stay usable for work, study, or music while the addictive surfaces disappear, CleanFeed is the better, cheaper, more precise fit — and its Focus Lock and Pomodoro give you real commitment without nuking the whole site. Plenty of people run Cold Turkey for scheduled deep-work blackouts and keep CleanFeed on underneath for the in-between hours when YouTube has to stay open but tidy. That coexistence isn't a compromise; it's the two tools doing what each does best. CleanFeed is new and its day-one user base is small, but it is open source (MIT) so you can read exactly what it does — and at $4.99 it's a low-risk way to find out whether surgical beats nuclear for you.
